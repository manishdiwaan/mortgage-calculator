## Understanding the Norway Property Market

The property market in Norway offers diverse opportunities across its key markets. Oslo, Bergen, Trondheim, Stavanger, and Tromsø represent the main buying hotspots, with prices and demand varying significantly between regions.

The Norges Bank sets the benchmark interest rate, which influences mortgage pricing across all lenders. Use our [mortgage calculator](/calculator/?country=no) to estimate repayments based on current rates.

## Property Prices and Key Markets

Typical prices in Norway range from kr 2,000,000 for apartments outside major cities to kr 5,000,000+ in central Oslo.

Norway's bidding process (budrunde) is uniquely transparent — all bids, including amounts and bidder identities, are recorded and made available to competing bidders. This prevents hidden bidding wars common in other markets. Norway also has a high rate of cooperative housing (borettslag), where you buy a share in the housing cooperative rather than the physical unit, which has different legal and tax implications

## Setting Your Budget

Beyond the purchase price, you need to budget for transaction costs that typically add 3-6% including document duty (2.5%, exempt for first-time buyers and borettslag), agent fees (paid by seller), and minor legal/registration costs to the total. Your deposit requirement is 15% minimum (regulatory requirement), though this varies by lender and your buyer profile.

Dokumentavgift (document duty) is 2.5% of the property's market value. First-time buyers and co-op (borettslag) purchases are exempt from this tax

## Getting Finance Approved

Mortgage terms in Norway extend up to 30 years, though 25 years is standard. Lenders typically offer up to Maximum 85% (Norwegian Financial Supervisory Authority regulation). In Oslo, a stricter 60% LTV applies to secondary/investment properties. Minimum 15% equity required for all purchases loan-to-value, meaning you need at least the remainder as a deposit.

Compare rates across major lenders including DNB (largest), Nordea, SpareBank 1, Handelsbanken, Danske Bank, and various local savings banks (sparebanker). Our [mortgage types guide](/norway/blog/mortgage-types-compared/) explains the differences between available loan products.

## Finding and Securing Property

Working with a qualified estate agent (eiendomsmegler) is the standard approach in Norway. Research local areas thoroughly, attend viewings, and consider factors like transport links, amenities, and future development plans.

## Legal Process and Due Diligence

Property transfers in Norway require a qualified the estate agent handles legal aspects (lawyers are optional but available) to handle the legal process. Norwegian property sales are well-regulated. The seller's agent arranges viewings and an open bidding process (budrunde) — bidding is transparent, with all bids logged and available to other bidders. The buyer must allow 24 hours for the seller to consider each bid. Once accepted, the purchase is binding. The megler (agent) handles the contract, payment transfer through a client account, and registration with the Kartverket (land registry).

The seller provides a tilstandsrapport (condition report) prepared by a certified building inspector. Buyers can also commission their own inspection. Norwegian law provides a 5-year warranty period on property defects through the avhendingslova (Property Sales Act), which was strengthened in 2022 to eliminate 'as-is' sales

See our [property inspection checklist](/norway/blog/property-inspection-checklist/) for what to check before committing.

## Government Support for Buyers

Norway has limited direct buyer subsidies. First-time buyers are exempt from the 2.5% document duty. Husbanken (the Norwegian State Housing Bank) provides start loans (startlån) for buyers who cannot obtain conventional financing — typically young buyers, single parents, or those with low income. BSU (boligsparing for ungdom) was a tax-advantaged savings scheme for young buyers that offered a 20% tax deduction on savings up to kr 27,500/year
