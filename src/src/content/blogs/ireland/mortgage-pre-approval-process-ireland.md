## What Mortgage Pre-Approval Actually Means in Ireland

Before you attend a single property viewing or make an offer on a home, one document will define your entire buying journey: your **Approval in Principle (AIP)**. In Ireland, this is the standard term lenders use for what is commonly called mortgage pre-approval elsewhere, and understanding precisely what it means — and what it does not mean — is essential for any serious buyer in 2026.

An AIP is a conditional written statement from a lender confirming that, based on the information you have provided, they are prepared to lend you up to a specified amount. It is not a legally binding mortgage offer. It does not guarantee funds will be released. But in Ireland's competitive property market, where bidding wars are common in Dublin, Cork, and Galway, estate agents and vendors treat a valid AIP as proof that you are a credible buyer. Without one, your offer on any property will rarely be taken seriously.

This guide walks you through every step of obtaining an AIP in Ireland, the Central Bank lending rules that shape what you can borrow, what lenders actually scrutinise, and how to maximise your chances of approval.

## The Central Bank Mortgage Lending Rules: Your Starting Point

Ireland's mortgage market operates under **macroprudential lending rules** set by the Central Bank of Ireland. Revised significantly in late 2023 and continuing into 2026, these rules form the hard ceiling on what any regulated lender can offer you, regardless of your income.

### The Two Key Limits

**1. Loan-to-Value (LTV) Ratio**
For first-time buyers (FTBs), the maximum LTV is **90%**, meaning you must have at least a 10% deposit. If you are purchasing a property worth €350,000, you need a minimum of €35,000 as a deposit before your mortgage can proceed.

For second and subsequent buyers, the LTV limit is stricter at **80%**, requiring a 20% deposit on the property's purchase price.

**2. Loan-to-Income (LTI) Ratio**
This is the ratio that determines your maximum borrowing ceiling relative to your gross annual income. Since the Central Bank reforms, FTBs can borrow up to **4 times** their gross income. Second and subsequent buyers are capped at **3.5 times** gross income.

For a couple with a combined gross income of €90,000, the maximum loan available as first-time buyers is €360,000. This is a hard regulatory limit, not a bank policy — no Irish lender regulated by the Central Bank can exceed it without using one of the permitted exemptions (which are limited in number and highly competitive to obtain).

These rules mean your first practical step, even before approaching a lender, is calculating your own numbers honestly.

## Step 1: Know Your Numbers Before Applying

Irish lenders do not simply look at your headline salary. They assess **net disposable income** — what remains after tax, PRSI, USC, existing loan repayments, and essential living costs. Before you approach any bank or broker, gather the following:

- **P60s or Employment Detail Summaries** for the past two years (available via MyAccount on Revenue.ie)
- **Six months of current account bank statements** — all accounts, including savings
- **Recent payslips** (typically three months for PAYE workers)
- **Evidence of your deposit** — source of funds matters enormously; lenders want to see it building over time, not appearing suddenly
- **Details of any existing loans**, car finance, credit union loans, or credit card balances
- **If self-employed:** two years of audited accounts and a current tax clearance certificate from Revenue

Self-employed applicants face additional scrutiny. Lenders typically average the last two years of net profit (not revenue) and will request a Form 11 tax return and a letter from your accountant confirming the business is sustainable.

---

## Step 2: Check Your Credit Record

Before a lender checks your credit history, you should. In Ireland, credit data is held by the **Central Credit Register (CCR)**, operated by the Central Bank. You are legally entitled to request a free copy of your credit report at any time via **centralcreditregister.ie**.

The CCR holds information on any loan of €500 or more taken out since June 2017. Lenders will look for:

- Missed or late payments on any existing credit
- Defaults or judgments
- High utilisation of revolving credit (such as credit cards)
- Patterns of financial stress

A common issue Irish buyers overlook is **credit card behaviour**. Even if you clear your balance monthly, a card with a high limit counts as a potential liability. Many mortgage advisors recommend reducing or closing unused credit card facilities three to six months before applying.

Ireland does not have a formal credit scoring system like the US FICO model. Each lender conducts its own assessment, but adverse entries on the CCR — particularly within the past 24 months — will significantly weaken your application.

## Step 3: Choosing a Lender or Mortgage Broker

In Ireland in 2026, your main retail mortgage lenders include **Bank of Ireland**, **AIB (Allied Irish Banks)**, **PTSB (Permanent TSB)**, **Avant Money**, and **Finance Ireland**. Avant Money, in particular, has been competitive on fixed rates and has attracted significant market attention since entering the market.

You can approach lenders directly or work through a **mortgage broker**. A broker (who must be authorised by the Central Bank under the Consumer Protection Code) has access to multiple lenders and can match your profile to the most suitable products. For applications that are complex — self-employment, contract workers, non-EEA nationals with Irish residency — a good broker is often worth the time saved.

Regardless of which route you choose, you will complete a **mortgage application form** that covers your personal details, employment, income, existing financial commitments, property details (if known), and intended purchase price.

---

## Step 4: The Lender's Assessment Process

Once your application is submitted with supporting documents, the lender's underwriting team will assess:

**Income Verification**
Lenders apply what is called a **stress test** — they calculate whether you could still afford repayments if interest rates rose by 2% above the rate you are applying for. This ensures borrowers are not overextended should rates increase during the mortgage term.

**Affordability Assessment**
Under the **Consumer Protection Code 2012** (updated guidelines), lenders must assess that the mortgage is affordable and suitable. They will calculate a net monthly surplus after all commitments and living expenses. If the surplus is too thin, even a technically eligible applicant may be declined.

**Rental History**
If you are currently renting and can provide 12 months of consistent rent payment records — particularly through a bank statement showing regular direct debits — this can strengthen your application by demonstrating financial reliability.

**The AIP Decision**
Most Irish lenders will issue an AIP decision within **5 to 10 business days** of receiving a complete application. AIB and Bank of Ireland have invested in faster digital processing, with some straightforward applications receiving conditional approval within 48 hours through online portals.

## What Your AIP Letter Contains

Your AIP letter will specify:

- The maximum loan amount approved in principle
- The indicative interest rate or rate type (fixed/variable)
- The term of the mortgage (typically up to 35 years for FTBs)
- The conditions attached — usually including a satisfactory property valuation, confirmation of employment, and title confirmation via a solicitor
- The **validity period** — in Ireland, most AIPs are valid for **6 months**, after which you must apply for a renewal (which will require updated documentation)

In a rising-rate environment, do not assume the rate referenced in your AIP will remain available at drawdown. Rates can change between AIP and drawdown, so monitor lender announcements carefully.

---

## After AIP: From Offer Accepted to Mortgage Drawdown

Once you have your AIP and an offer accepted on a property, the process moves to **full mortgage approval**. This requires:

1. A **property valuation** by a lender-approved valuer (costs approximately €150–€200)
2. Your **solicitor's undertaking** confirming title and legal searches
3. **Mortgage protection insurance** — mandatory in Ireland under the Consumer Credit Act 1995; lenders cannot drawdown without it (exemptions exist for those over 50 or with serious medical conditions, subject to lender discretion)
4. **Home insurance** evidence

The lender then issues a formal **Letter of Offer** — the legally binding mortgage contract. Your solicitor will review this before you sign. Drawdown typically follows within a few weeks of signing, coordinated with the closing date agreed with the vendor.

## Common Reasons AIP Applications Are Declined in Ireland

Understanding why applications fail helps you avoid the same pitfalls:

- **Insufficient deposit** with no satisfactory evidence of savings history
- **Recent job change** (lenders typically want 6–12 months in your current role; probationary periods are problematic)
- **High existing debt** levels eating into disposable income
- **Irregular income patterns** — large unexplained lodgements or withdrawals on bank statements raise questions
- **Exceeding the LTI ratio** for the price of property you are targeting

If declined, you have the right under the **Consumer Protection Code** to receive a clear explanation and to appeal the decision internally before seeking review through the **Financial Services and Pensions Ombudsman (FSPO)**.

## Practical Tips to Strengthen Your Application

- **Start 12 months out.** Use the 12 months before application to build a clean financial record — consistent savings, minimal discretionary spending on statements, no missed payments.
- **Use a **Help to Buy (HTB)** refund strategically.** If you qualify (purchasing a new build under €500,000), the Revenue HTB scheme can refund up to €30,000 in income tax paid over four years. This boosts your deposit but requires a revenue.ie application and AIP from a qualifying lender.
- **Get multiple AIPs if possible.** There is no prohibition on applying to multiple lenders simultaneously, and having competing offers gives you options if one lender's rates change.
- **Do not make major financial changes** between AIP and drawdown — avoid new car finance, changing jobs, or making large cash withdrawals without documentation.

---

## The Bottom Line

Ireland's mortgage pre-approval process is more structured and regulatory-driven than in many other countries, largely because of the Central Bank's macroprudential rules. Understanding those rules, preparing your documentation meticulously, and presenting a clean financial profile to lenders will put you in the strongest possible position in a market where the difference between an accepted and rejected offer often comes down to the credibility of your AIP letter.

Start your preparation early, consider engaging a Central Bank-authorised broker, and treat the process as a financial audit of your own situation — because that is precisely what lenders are conducting.
