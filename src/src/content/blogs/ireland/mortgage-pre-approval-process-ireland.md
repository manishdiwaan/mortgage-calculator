## What Is Mortgage Pre-Approval in Ireland — and Why Does It Matter?

If you are serious about buying property in Ireland, getting mortgage pre-approval is not just a helpful first step — it is effectively a prerequisite. Estate agents across Dublin, Cork, Galway, and beyond will rarely take an offer seriously unless you can produce an **Approval in Principle (AIP)** letter. In a market where desirable properties in areas like Ranelagh, Blackrock, or the Galway docks regularly attract multiple bidders within days of listing, arriving without documented financial readiness is a significant competitive disadvantage.

Yet many prospective buyers — including those with strong incomes and solid savings — enter this process underprepared, misunderstanding what pre-approval actually means, how long it lasts, and how Central Bank lending rules shape what any lender can offer them. This guide walks you through the entire process in plain terms, with specific reference to how Irish lenders, regulators, and market conditions work in 2026.

---

## Approval in Principle vs. Full Mortgage Approval: Know the Difference

These two terms are often confused, and conflating them can lead to costly mistakes.

**Approval in Principle (AIP)** is a conditional, non-binding assessment by a lender indicating that, based on information you have provided, they would likely lend you a specified amount. It is based primarily on your income, employment status, and credit profile. It does **not** account for the specific property you intend to buy. Most AIPs in Ireland are valid for **6 to 12 months**, depending on the lender.

**Full Mortgage Approval** (also called formal or loan offer) only comes after you have identified a specific property, had it valued by the lender's approved valuer, and submitted the full documentation pack — including the signed sale agreed contract from your solicitor. This is the binding offer that triggers the drawdown process.

Understanding this distinction matters because some buyers waste their AIP window on extended property searches, only to find their letter has lapsed when they finally go sale agreed. Others mistakenly believe their AIP guarantees the loan — it does not.

## Central Bank Mortgage Lending Rules in 2026

The **Central Bank of Ireland** sets macro-prudential mortgage lending rules that every regulated lender in Ireland must follow. These rules cap both how much you can borrow relative to your income and how much you can borrow relative to the property's value. As of 2026, the key limits are:

### Loan-to-Income (LTI) Limit
- **First-time buyers:** Can borrow up to **4 times** their gross annual income
- **Second and subsequent buyers:** Also capped at **4 times** gross income as of the 2023 rule revision, which aligned limits across buyer categories

For a couple with a combined gross income of €120,000, this means a maximum loan of **€480,000** — regardless of how much deposit they have beyond the minimum.

Lenders are permitted to make a limited number of exceptions above this threshold — up to **20% of new lending** to first-time buyers and **10%** to second-and-subsequent buyers annually can exceed the LTI cap. These exceptions are competitive and are typically reserved for borrowers with very strong credit profiles, stable high-income professions, or significant additional assets.

### Loan-to-Value (LTV) Limit
- **First-time buyers:** Maximum LTV of **90%**, meaning a minimum **10% deposit** is required
- **Second and subsequent buyers:** Maximum LTV of **80%**, requiring at least a **20% deposit**
- **Buy-to-let buyers:** Maximum LTV of **70%**

The **Help to Buy (HTB) scheme**, administered by Revenue, can contribute up to **€30,000** (or 10% of the purchase price, whichever is lower) toward a first-time buyer's deposit on a new build. This can significantly assist with meeting the 10% LTV requirement. The First Home Scheme, operated jointly by the State and participating lenders, can bridge a further gap in equity for new builds and self-builds.

## Step-by-Step: The Irish Mortgage Pre-Approval Process

### Step 1: Assess Your Borrowing Capacity

Before approaching any lender, do your own honest assessment. Use the Central Bank's LTI and LTV rules as your framework, not just a lender's online calculator (which may show optimistic figures before exceptions and stress tests are applied).

Factor in:
- Your **gross annual income** (basic salary only — most lenders discount overtime, bonuses, and commission income significantly, often by 50%)
- Your **existing debts** — car finance, personal loans, credit card balances, and even BNPL (buy now, pay later) facilities are scrutinised
- Your **monthly outgoings** — lenders will stress test your repayments at a rate typically **2% above the current rate** to ensure you can still afford the mortgage if rates rise

### Step 2: Review Your Credit Report

Obtain a copy of your credit record from the **Central Credit Register (CCR)**, which is maintained by the Central Bank of Ireland. You are entitled to one free report per year at **centralcreditregister.ie**. Unlike some countries, Ireland does not use a single numerical credit score — lenders review the raw payment history data themselves.

Check for:
- Any missed or late payments on loans or credit cards
- Undeclared credit facilities you may have forgotten
- Errors in your record (these must be formally disputed with the CCR)

Even a single missed payment within the past 12 months can result in an automatic decline at some lenders. Address any issues before applying.

### Step 3: Gather Your Documentation

Irish lenders are thorough. Expect to submit:

- **6 months of current account bank statements** — lenders will review every transaction for gambling activity, undisclosed loans, or irregular financial behaviour
- **6 months of savings account statements** — to demonstrate consistent saving habits and verify deposit accumulation
- **Payslips** — typically the most recent 3 months for PAYE employees
- **P60 or Employment Detail Summary** from Revenue (accessible via MyAccount on revenue.ie)
- **If self-employed:** 2–3 years of certified accounts from an accountant, plus recent tax returns (Form 11)
- **Photo ID and proof of address**
- **Evidence of any gifts** — a gift letter is required if part of your deposit is gifted, confirming it is not a loan

### Step 4: Apply to Multiple Lenders

Ireland's mortgage market in 2026 includes **AIB, Bank of Ireland, Permanent TSB, Haven Mortgages** (EBS/AIB), **ICS Mortgages**, **Finance Ireland**, and **Avant Money**, among others. Rates, cashback offers, and underwriting criteria vary meaningfully between them.

You can apply directly to each lender or use a **mortgage broker**, which can be advantageous. Brokers with access to the whole market — such as those regulated under the **Consumer Credit Act 1995** and authorised by the Central Bank — can compare live rates across lenders and advocate on your behalf during underwriting. Broker fees are sometimes charged to the client, but many operate on lender commission only. Confirm the arrangement upfront.

### Step 5: Receive and Understand Your AIP Letter

When a lender issues an AIP, read it carefully. It will state:
- The **maximum loan amount** approved
- The **validity period** (commonly 6 or 12 months)
- Any **conditions** — such as a requirement that employment remain continuous, or that savings not decrease below a stated level

Conditions matter. If you change jobs during your AIP period, you should notify your lender — a probationary period in a new role may suspend your AIP entirely.

## Common Pitfalls in the Pre-Approval Stage

### Taking on New Debt After AIP
Any new credit — a car loan, a new credit card, even a mobile phone contract with a credit check — can alter your financial profile and cause the lender to reassess or withdraw an offer. Avoid all new financial commitments until drawdown is complete.

### Switching Jobs During the Process
Lenders in Ireland typically require **minimum continuous employment of 6 months** (or 12 months if previously on a probationary period). Moving roles mid-application is one of the most common reasons for delays and refusals.

### Underestimating Costs Beyond the Deposit
Your AIP focuses on the loan, but you must also fund:
- **Solicitor's fees:** €1,500–€3,000 plus VAT
- **Valuation fee:** €150–€200 (required by the lender)
- **Surveyor's fee:** €400–€800 (independent structural survey, strongly recommended)
- **Stamp duty:** 1% on residential properties up to €1 million
- **Land Registry fees**

Budget at least **€3,000–€5,000** in transaction costs above your deposit.

## How Long Does the Full Process Take?

From initial AIP application to mortgage drawdown, the typical timeline in Ireland runs:

| Stage | Typical Duration |
|---|---|
| AIP application to decision | 2–4 weeks |
| Property search (sale agreed) | Variable (weeks to months) |
| Full mortgage application after sale agreed | 2–4 weeks |
| Legal process (conveyancing) | 6–12 weeks |
| **Total (estimate)** | **3–6 months** |

New builds and self-builds typically take longer due to stage payments, planning compliance, and snagging processes.

## Final Advice: Start Early and Stay Organised

The Irish property market does not reward hesitation. Starting your pre-approval process 3–6 months before you intend to bid gives you time to address credit issues, build savings, and engage with multiple lenders without pressure. Keep a dedicated folder — physical or digital — with every financial document from the past 2 years. Lenders will ask for it, and being prepared signals seriousness.

If in doubt, consult a **Central Bank-authorised mortgage broker** or an independent financial advisor regulated under the **Investment Intermediaries Act 1995**. Their fee is typically far smaller than the cost of a rushed decision on what is likely the largest financial commitment of your life.
