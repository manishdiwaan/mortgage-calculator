## What Is Japan's Mortgage Pre-Approval Process and Why Does It Matter?

If you are serious about buying property in Japan, understanding the mortgage pre-approval system is not optional — it is the foundation of your entire purchasing strategy. In Japan, the formal pre-approval step is called **jizen shinsa** (事前審査), which translates literally as "prior examination." This preliminary screening is a distinct and mandatory stage that happens before the main loan examination, known as **honshinsa** (本審査), and it carries far more weight in the Japanese property market than equivalent processes in countries like Australia, the United States, or the United Kingdom.

Real estate agents in Japan — particularly those handling new-build condominiums (shin-chiku manshon) and high-demand properties in central Tokyo, Osaka, and Nagoya — will typically require a completed jizen shinsa result before they will even allow a buyer to submit a formal purchase application. In competitive submarkets such as Minato-ku or Shibuya-ku in Tokyo, where well-priced properties attract multiple offers, arriving without a pre-approval is the fastest way to lose out.

This guide walks you through every stage of the process, the documents you will need, the eligibility criteria Japanese lenders apply, and the strategic decisions that can strengthen your position before you ever sit across from a loan officer.

## Stage One: Jizen Shinsa — The Preliminary Examination

The jizen shinsa is conducted by the financial institution you intend to borrow from and typically takes **three to five business days** to complete, though some banks — particularly Japan's large megabanks like **MUFG Bank (三菱UFJ銀行)**, **Mizuho Bank (みずほ銀行)**, and **SMBC (三井住友銀行)** — can return results in as little as two business days for straightforward applications.

During this stage, the lender performs a preliminary check of your financial health, employment status, credit record, and the property itself. Crucially, the jizen shinsa does **not** result in a credit inquiry that appears on your formal credit record in most cases, which means you can approach multiple lenders during this stage without the same risk of inquiry accumulation that exists in other markets.

### What Lenders Assess During Jizen Shinsa

**Annual Income and Repayment Ratio (返済比率 — Hensai Hiritsu)**

Japanese lenders apply strict annual repayment ratio caps. Most banks will not approve loans where total annual debt repayments (including any existing car loans, credit card installments, or student loans) exceed **25% to 35% of gross annual income**. For applicants earning ¥5,000,000 per year, this means total annual mortgage repayments are typically capped at ¥1,250,000 to ¥1,750,000 — a figure that directly determines your maximum borrowing ceiling.

Some institutions, particularly the **Japan Housing Finance Agency (住宅金融支援機構 — Jutaku Kinyu Shien Kiko)**, which administers the government-backed **Flat 35** fixed-rate mortgage product, apply a tiered hensai hiritsu structure: 30% for applicants earning under ¥4,000,000 annually, and 35% for those earning above that threshold.

**Employment Type and Continuity**

Japanese lenders draw a firm distinction between **seishain** (正社員 — permanent full-time employees), **keiyaku shain** (契約社員 — contract employees), **jieigyosha** (自営業者 — self-employed individuals), and **haken shain** (派遣社員 — temporary agency workers). Permanent employees of established companies receive the most favorable treatment. Self-employed applicants typically need to demonstrate at least **three consecutive years of stable declared income** through tax returns (kakuteishinkoku), and lenders will often average the three years rather than using the most recent year's figure.

Contract workers and dispatched employees face the most scrutiny. Some banks will decline outright; others will consider applications if the current contract has been renewed multiple times and the employer is a recognized large corporation.

**Credit Record via CIC and JICC**

Japan has three main credit information agencies: **CIC (Credit Information Center)**, **JICC (Japan Credit Information Reference Center Corp)**, and the **KSC (全国銀行個人信用情報センター)** managed by the Japanese Bankers Association. Lenders will check your record across these bureaus for late payments, defaults, or excessive outstanding credit balances. Even a single 61-day-late payment on a credit card within the past five years can be sufficient grounds for rejection at conservative institutions.

## Stage Two: Document Preparation — What You Actually Need

Document requirements vary by lender, but the following represents the core package required by the majority of major Japanese financial institutions:

- **Honnin kakunin shorui** (本人確認書類): Government-issued photo ID — typically My Number card (個人番号カード) or driver's license
- **Zeikin kanren shorui** (税金関連書類): Most recent **nozei shomeisho** (納税証明書 — tax payment certificate) and **shotoku shomeisho** (所得証明書 — income certificate), both obtainable from your local municipal office (shiyakusho or kuyakusho)
- **Hokenho** (保険証): Health insurance card, which confirms employer affiliation
- **Gensen choshuhyo** (源泉徴収票): Annual withholding tax statement issued by your employer (equivalent to a payment summary or W-2)
- **Kinyu kikan no tsuchosho** (金融機関の通帳): Bank passbook or recent bank statements for the past three to six months, demonstrating savings history
- **Bukken shiryo** (物件資料): Property documentation including the sales contract draft (baikaitorihiki keiyakusho), floor plan (madori), and property registration details (toukijikou shomeisho)

For foreign nationals purchasing property in Japan — which is legally permitted without restriction under Japan's real estate laws — additional documentation is typically required, including your **zairyu card** (在留カード — residence card), a copy of your passport, and evidence of continuous residence in Japan. Foreign nationals on long-term visas (permanent residence, spouse of Japanese national, long-term resident) generally receive treatment comparable to Japanese citizens. Those on shorter-term working visas may face higher scrutiny and are sometimes limited to specific lenders, including some regional banks (chiho ginko) and credit unions (shinkin banks) with international borrower programs.

## Stage Three: Choosing the Right Lender Before You Apply

The jizen shinsa landscape in Japan offers genuine strategic choices. The three main lender categories — megabanks, regional banks, and the Flat 35 program — each have distinct profiles:

### Megabanks (MUFG, Mizuho, SMBC, Resona)
Offer competitive variable rates, currently ranging from approximately **0.3% to 0.5% per annum** as of early 2026 on their lowest variable products, following the Bank of Japan's gradual policy rate normalization. Approval criteria are relatively strict. Best suited to applicants with seishain employment at large corporations, clean credit records, and incomes above ¥5,000,000.

### Regional Banks and Shinkin Banks
Often more flexible with self-employed applicants and those with non-standard employment. Rates are slightly higher, typically **0.5% to 0.9%** on variable products, but approval decisions may incorporate more qualitative assessment of the applicant's local community ties and business history.

### Flat 35 (Japan Housing Finance Agency)
Japan's signature government-backed fixed-rate product offers full fixed-rate certainty for terms of 15 to 35 years. As of early 2026, benchmark Flat 35 rates sit between approximately **1.8% and 2.3%** depending on term and loan-to-value ratio. The Flat 35S variant offers a discounted rate for properties that meet higher energy efficiency and safety standards under the **Housing Quality Assurance Act (住宅の品質確保の促進等に関する法律)**. This product is administered through approved partner financial institutions (tekigai kinyu kikan) rather than directly.

## Stage Four: From Pre-Approval to Honshinsya

Once jizen shinsa is approved, you typically have **30 to 90 days** to complete a purchase agreement and move into the formal honshinsya process. The honshinsya is a more comprehensive examination that includes a formal property appraisal, legal title search, and verification of all submitted documents against original sources. This stage usually takes **one to two weeks**.

A critical point many buyers overlook: **jizen shinsa approval is not a guarantee of honshinsya approval**. If your financial situation changes between stages — a job change, a new car loan, or a significant reduction in your savings — the lender can and will reconsider. Experienced buyers and their agents strongly advise against making any major financial changes between jizen shinsa approval and loan settlement.

## Practical Tips for Strengthening Your Pre-Approval Position

1. **Pay down or close unused credit card accounts** before applying. Japanese lenders calculate credit limits as potential liability, not just current balances. A ¥500,000 credit card limit with a zero balance can still reduce your borrowing capacity.

2. **Avoid changing employers within 12 months of your planned purchase.** Even a move to a higher-paying position at a more prestigious company creates uncertainty in lenders' eyes. If a job change is unavoidable, many buyers choose to apply before the transition is formalized.

3. **Build a documented savings history, not just a lump sum.** Japanese lenders look favorably on applicants whose bank statements show disciplined, consistent monthly savings over 12 to 24 months. A large cash gift deposited shortly before application raises more questions than it answers.

4. **Consider applying through a mortgage broker (hoken yaosan or jutaku loan aite-tsugi service).** Services like **ARUHI Corporation** or **SBI Mortgage** specialize in matching applicants with appropriate lenders and can often identify institutions more likely to approve non-standard profiles.

5. **Check your credit record before the lender does.** You can request your own credit report from CIC online for a fee of ¥1,000, which allows you to identify and dispute any errors before they affect your application.

## The Bottom Line

Japan's jizen shinsa system is methodical, conservative, and deeply document-driven — but once you understand its logic, it becomes a powerful tool rather than an obstacle. Buyers who prepare their documentation thoroughly, understand how hensai hiritsu affects their borrowing ceiling, and make strategic choices about timing and lender selection consistently achieve better outcomes than those who approach the process reactively.

In a market where interest rates are beginning to normalize after decades near zero, and where attractive properties in well-located urban areas continue to attract strong competition, securing a credible pre-approval is the single most effective step you can take to position yourself as a serious buyer.
